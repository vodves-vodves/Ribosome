The ribosome: a machine that reads

Every living cell contains thousands of ribosomes. They read messenger RNA three letters at a time and assemble proteins from amino acids.

A ribosome is made of two subunits. The small subunit decodes the message, while the large subunit links amino acids into a growing chain.

Bacterial ribosomes differ slightly from ours. That difference is why some antibiotics can stop bacteria without harming human cells.

Ribosomes are ancient. Their core has barely changed in three billion years, which makes them one of the most conserved structures in biology.

A single ribosome adds about twenty amino acids per second. A cell that needs a protein quickly simply puts more ribosomes on the same message.

Errors happen, but rarely: roughly one wrong amino acid in ten thousand. Proofreading steps catch most mistakes before the chain is released.

When the ribosome reaches a stop signal, it lets go. The finished protein folds, and the two subunits drift apart to look for the next message.

How the message is read

The message arrives as a strand of messenger RNA. Each group of three bases, called a codon, stands for one amino acid or for a stop.

Transfer RNA molecules do the matching. One end of each carries an anticodon that pairs with the message, the other end carries the amino acid.

The ribosome holds three of these molecules at once. As it steps forward by one codon, the chain is handed from one to the next.

A factory with no manager

Nobody tells a ribosome what to build. The order of codons is the only instruction it ever receives.

Yet the output is reliable enough to run a body for decades. Reliability comes from chemistry, not supervision.

A human cell can hold ten million ribosomes. In a growing bacterium they make up a quarter of its dry mass.

Why antibiotics work

Many antibiotics are simply molecules that jam the bacterial ribosome. Tetracycline blocks the slot where transfer RNA should land.

Erythromycin plugs the tunnel through which the new chain exits. The ribosome keeps reading, but nothing can leave.

Our own ribosomes are shaped differently, so the drugs mostly leave them alone. Mostly, not entirely: that gap is where side effects live.

What we still do not know

We can photograph a ribosome down to single atoms. We still cannot say exactly why it almost never slips by one base.

Some researchers believe the answer is in how the machine wobbles. Others think the message itself carries hidden rhythm.

Either way, the oldest reader on Earth still has pages we cannot turn.

Translation, in biology as in language, is the act of turning one alphabet into another.
